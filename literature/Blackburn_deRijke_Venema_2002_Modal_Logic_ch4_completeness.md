## 4.5 Transforming the Canonical Model

*(Book pp. 219--225)*

What is the modal logic of partial orders? And what is the tense logic of strict total orders? Such questions bring us face to face with the fundamental problem confronting semantically driven completeness results. Partial orders are antisymmetric, and strict total orders are irreflexive. No modal formula defines either property, and no formula is canonical for them either. Thus, to answer either question, we need to build a model for which we lack a canonical formula --- and hence we will need to expand our repertoire of model building techniques.

In this section we explore a particularly natural strategy: **transforming the canonical model**. Although a canonical model may lack some desired properties, it does get a lot of things right. Perhaps it is possible to reshape it, transforming it into a model with all the desired properties? We study two transformations --- **unraveling** and **bulldozing** --- and use them to answer the questions above.

### Unraveling

**Definition 4.51 (Unraveling).** Let $(W, R)$ be a frame generated by some point $w \in W$. The *unraveling* of $(W, R)$ around $w$ is the frame $(\widetilde{W}, \widetilde{R})$ where:

(i) $\widetilde{W}$ is the set of all finite sequences $(w, w_1, \ldots, w_n)$ such that $w_1, \ldots, w_n \in W$ and $Rww_1, \ldots, Rw_{n-1}w_n$, and

(ii) If $\widetilde{s}_1, \widetilde{s}_2 \in \widetilde{W}$, then $\widetilde{R}\widetilde{s}_1\widetilde{s}_2$ if there is some $v \in W$ such that $\widetilde{s}_1 \mathbin{+} (v) = \widetilde{s}_2$, where $+$ denotes sequence concatenation.

If $M = (W, R, V)$ is a model and $(\widetilde{W}, \widetilde{R})$ is the unraveling of $(W, R)$ around $w$, then we define the valuation $\widetilde{V}$ on $(\widetilde{W}, \widetilde{R})$ as follows:

$$\widetilde{V}(p) = \{(w, w_1, \ldots, w_n) \in \widetilde{W} \mid w_n \in V(p)\}$$

The model $\widetilde{M} = (\widetilde{W}, \widetilde{R}, \widetilde{V})$ is called the *unraveling of $M$ around $w$*.

Unraveled frames are trees: the root node is the sequence $(w)$, and $\widetilde{R}$ is the familiar (immediate) successor (daughter-of) relation on trees.

**Lemma 4.52.** Let $\widetilde{M} = (\widetilde{W}, \widetilde{R}, \widetilde{V})$ be the unraveling of $M = (W, R, V)$ around $w$. Then $(W, R)$ is a bounded morphic image of $(\widetilde{W}, \widetilde{R})$, and $M$ is a bounded morphic image of $\widetilde{M}$.

*Proof.* Let $f : \widetilde{W} \to W$ be defined by $f(w, w_1, \ldots, w_n) = w_n$. It is easy to see that $f$ is surjective, has the back and forth property, and that for any $\widetilde{s} \in \widetilde{W}$, $\widetilde{s}$ and $f(\widetilde{s})$ satisfy the same propositional variables. $\square$

A simple corollary is that any satisfiable set of formulas is satisfiable on a (irreflexive, intransitive, and asymmetric) tree. It follows that $\mathbf{K}$ is (strongly) complete with respect to this class of models.

### Unraveling for S4

**Lemma 4.53.** Let $M = (W, R, V)$ be a reflexive transitive model generated by some $w \in W$, and let $(\widetilde{W}, \widetilde{R}, \widetilde{V})$ be the unraveling of $M$ around $w$. Let $R^*$ be the reflexive transitive closure of $\widetilde{R}$, and define $M^* = (\widetilde{W}, R^*, \widetilde{V})$. Then $M$ is a bounded morphic image of $M^*$.

*Proof.* The function $f$ defined in Lemma 4.52 remains the required bounded morphism; as far as surjectivity, the back property, and the distribution of proposition letters are concerned, nothing has changed. We only have to check that taking the reflexive transitive closure of $\widetilde{R}$ does not harm the forth property. But, as $R$ is itself reflexive and transitive, the forth property survives. $\square$

**Theorem 4.54.** $\mathbf{S4}$ is strongly complete with respect to the class of partially ordered reflexive and transitive trees.

*Proof.* If $\Sigma$ is an S4-consistent set of formulas, and $\Gamma^+$ is an S4-MCS extending $\Sigma$, then $M_{\mathbf{S4}}, \Gamma^+ \Vdash \Sigma$. Moreover, as the S4 axioms are canonical, $M_{\mathbf{S4}}$ is a reflexive transitive model. We now transform this model into the required partial order in two steps.

*Step 1.* Let $M_S$ be the submodel of $M_{\mathbf{S4}}$ generated by $\Gamma^+$. This is a reflexive, transitive, point-generated model such that $M_S, \Gamma^+ \Vdash \Sigma$.

*Step 2.* Let $M^* = (\widetilde{W}, R^*, \widetilde{V})$ be the reflexive transitive closure of the unraveling of $M_S$ around $\Gamma^+$.

By Lemma 4.53, $M_S$ is a bounded morphic image of $M^*$ under $f$, hence for all sequences $\widetilde{s} \in f^{-1}[\Sigma]$, we have $M^*, \widetilde{s} \Vdash \Sigma$, and by the surjectivity of $f$ there is at least one such $\widetilde{s}$. Hence we have satisfied $\Sigma$ on a reflexive and transitive tree. $\square$

### Bulldozing

**Definition 4.55.** Let $(T, R)$ be a transitive frame. A *cluster* on $(T, R)$ is a subset $C$ of $T$ that is a maximal equivalence relation under $R$. That is, the restriction of $R$ to $C$ is an equivalence relation, and this is not the case for any other subset $D$ of $T$ such that $C \subset D$. A cluster is *simple* if it consists of a single reflexive point, and *proper* if it contains more than one point.

Any transitive trichotomous frame can be thought of as a strictly totally ordered collection of clusters. If we could remove each cluster as a single chunk, and replace it with something equivalent, we would have performed a local model transformation.

The key question is: what should we replace clusters with? Clearly some sort of STO --- but how can we do this in a truth preserving way? Note that any cluster $C$, even a simple one, introduces an infinity of information recurrence in both the forward and backward directions. Thus, when we replace a cluster $C$ with a STO, we must ensure that the STO duplicates all the information in $C$ infinitely often, in both directions. Bulldozing does precisely this: we impose a strict total order on the cluster, then lay out infinitely many copies of this path in both the forward and backward direction.

**Theorem 4.56.** $\mathbf{K_t4.3}$ is strongly complete with respect to the class of strict total orders.

*Proof.* Let $\Sigma$ be a $\mathbf{K_t4.3}$-consistent set of formulas; expand it to a $\mathbf{K_t4.3}$-MCS $\Gamma^+$. Let $M = (T, R, V)$ be the canonical model for $\mathbf{K_t4.3}$. By the canonicity of the axioms, $M$ is transitive and non-branching. Let $M_S = (S, R_S, V_S)$ be the submodel of $M$ generated by $\Gamma^+$; $M_S$ is a transitive and trichotomous model such that $M_S, \Gamma^+ \Vdash \Sigma$. But $M_S$ may contain clusters, which we will bulldoze away.

*Step 1.* Index the clusters in $M_S$ by some suitable set $I$.

*Step 2.* Define an arbitrary strict total order $<_i$ on each cluster $C_i$.

*Step 3.* Define $C_i^* = C_i \times \mathbb{Z}$.

*Step 4.* Define $B$, the set underlying the bulldozed model, to be $S^- \cup \bigcup_{i \in I} C_i^*$, where $S^- = S \setminus \bigcup_{i \in I} C_i$ is the set of points not belonging to any cluster.

*Step 5.* Define a mapping $\pi : B \to S$ by: $\pi(b) = b$ if $b \in S^-$; and $\pi(b) = s$ if $b = (s, z)$.

*Step 6.* Define an ordering $<_b$ on $B$ by $b <_b b'$ iff:
- either ($b \in S^-$ or $b' \in S^-$) and $\pi(b) R_S \pi(b')$;
- or $b = (s, z)$ and $b' = (s', z')$ and either:
  - $s$ and $s'$ belong to distinct clusters and $\pi(b) R_S \pi(b')$;
  - or $s$ and $s'$ belong to the same cluster and $z <_{\mathbb{Z}} z'$;
  - or $s$ and $s'$ belong to the same cluster $C_i$ and $z = z'$ and $s <_i s'$.

*Step 7.* Define a valuation $V_b$ on $(B, <_b)$ by $b \in V(p)$ iff $\pi(b) \in V_S(p)$.

*Step 8.* Define $M_B$, the bulldozed model, to be $(B, <_b, V_b)$.

**Claim 1.** The mapping $\pi$ is a surjective bounded morphism from $(B, <_b)$ to $(S, R_S)$, and the model $M_S$ is a bounded morphic image of $M_B$ under $\pi$.

**Claim 2.** $(B, <_b)$ is a strict total order.

With this done, the theorem is immediate. By Claim 1, for any $b \in \pi^{-1}(\Gamma^+)$ we have $M_B, b \Vdash \Sigma$, and since $\pi$ is surjective, there is at least one such $b$. $\square$

## 4.6 Step-by-step

*(Book pp. 225--231)*

Three main ideas underlie the step-by-step method:

(i) Don't consider the entire canonical model to be the key ingredient of a completeness proof. Rather, think of selections of MCSs from the canonical model as the basic building blocks.

(ii) The standard way of proving completeness is by constructing a model for a consistent set of formulas. Take the term "constructing" as literally as possible: break it down into a sequence of steps.

(iii) Putting the first two observations together, think of the construction of a model as the stepwise selection of the needed MCSs. More precisely, think of the model construction process as approaching a limit via a sequence of ever better approximations, using local configurations of the canonical model to make improvements at each step of the construction.

To illustrate the method's potential, we use it to prove that the logic $\mathbf{K_tQ}$ is strongly complete with respect to $(\mathbb{Q}, <)$. In what follows, consistency means $\mathbf{K_tQ}$-consistency, and $M_c = (T_c, R_c, V_c)$ is this logic's canonical model. Furthermore we fix a maximal consistent set $\Gamma$; the goal of our proof is to construct a model $M = (T, <, V)$ for $\Gamma$ such that $(T, <)$ is an ordering isomorphic to $(\mathbb{Q}, <)$.

### Networks

**Definition 4.57.** A *network* is a triple $N = (N, R, \lambda)$ such that $R$ is a binary relation on the set $N$, and $\lambda$ is a labeling function mapping each point in $N$ to a maximal consistent set.

**Definition 4.58.** A network $N = (N, <, \lambda)$ is *coherent* if it satisfies:

- **(C1)** $<$ is a strict total ordering,
- **(C2)** $\lambda(s) R_c \lambda(t)$ for all $s, t \in N$ such that $s < t$.

A *network for $\Gamma$* is a network such that $\Gamma$ is the label set of some node.

C1 and C2 are the minimal requirements for a network to be useful; note that both are universal. (C2 is equivalent to the requirement that if $s < t$ then $F\varphi \in \lambda(s)$ for all $\varphi \in \lambda(t)$ and $P\varphi \in \lambda(t)$ for all $\varphi \in \lambda(s)$.)

### Saturation

**Definition 4.59.** A network $N = (N, <, \lambda)$ is *saturated* if it satisfies:

- **(S1)** $<$ is unbounded to the left and to the right,
- **(S2)** $<$ is dense,
- **(S3)** $N$ is *modally saturated*. That is, we demand that:
  - **(F)** if $F\psi \in \lambda(s)$ for some $s \in N$, then there is some $t \in N$ such that $Rst$ and $\psi \in \lambda(t)$, and
  - **(P)** if $P\psi \in \lambda(s)$ for some $s \in N$, then there is some $t \in N$ such that $Rts$ and $\psi \in \lambda(t)$.

A network is *perfect* if it is both coherent and saturated.

### Induced models

**Definition 4.60.** Let $N = (N, R, \lambda)$ be a network. The frame $F_N = (N, R)$ is the *underlying frame* of $N$. The *induced valuation* $V_N$ on $F$ is defined by $V_N(p) = \{s \in N \mid p \in \lambda(s)\}$. The structure $I_N = (F_N, V_N)$ is the *induced model*.

**Lemma 4.61 (Truth Lemma).** Let $N$ be a countably infinite perfect network. Then for all formulas $\psi$, and all nodes $s$ in $N$:

$$I_N, s \Vdash \psi \quad\text{iff}\quad \psi \in \lambda(s).$$

Moreover, $F_N$ is isomorphic to the ordering of the rational numbers.

*Proof.* The first part is by induction on the degree of $\psi$. The base case is clear from the definition of the induced valuation, and the steps for the booleans are straightforward. As for the modal operators, the coherency of $N$ drives the left to right implication, and saturation takes care of the other direction.

Finally, the underlying frame of a perfect network must be a dense, unbounded, strict total ordering. Hence, if it is countably infinite, it must be isomorphic to $(\mathbb{Q}, <)$ by Cantor's Theorem. $\square$

### Defects

**Definition 4.62.** Let $N = (N, R, \lambda)$ be a network. An *S1-defect* of $N$ consists of a node $s \in N$ that has no successor, or no predecessor; an *S2-defect* is a pair $(s, t)$ of nodes for which there is no intermediate point. An *S3-defect* consists of:

- **(F)** a node $s$ and a formula $F\psi \in \lambda(s)$ for which there is no $t$ in $N$ such that $Rst$ and $\psi \in \lambda(t)$, or
- **(P)** a node $s$ and a formula $P\psi \in \lambda(s)$ for which there is no $t$ in $N$ such that $Rts$ and $\psi \in \lambda(t)$.

**Definition 4.63.** Let $N_0$ and $N_1$ be two networks. We say that $N_1$ *extends* $N_0$ if $F_{N_0}$ is a subframe of $F_{N_1}$ and $\lambda_0$ agrees with $\lambda_1$ on $N_0$.

### The Repair Lemma

**Lemma 4.64 (Repair Lemma).** For any defect of a finite, coherent network $N$ there is a finite, coherent $N' \supseteq N$ lacking this defect.

*Proof.* Let $N = (N, <, \lambda)$ be a finite, coherent network with some defect.

**S1-defects.** Left as an exercise.

**S2-defects.** Assume that there are nodes $s$ and $t$ in $N$ for which there is no intermediate point. By coherence of $N$, $\lambda(s) R_c \lambda(t)$, and by canonicity of the density axiom there is some MCS $\Sigma$ such that $\lambda(s) R_c \Sigma R_c \lambda(t)$. Take some new node $u \notin N$ and define $N' = (N', <', \lambda')$ by:

$$N' := N \cup \{u\}; \quad <' \;:=\; < \;\cup\; \{(x,u) \mid x \leq s\} \cup \{(u,x) \mid t \leq x\}; \quad \lambda' := \lambda \cup \{(u, \Sigma)\}.$$

It is clear that $N'$ is a network that does not suffer from the old defect. Condition C1 is almost immediate. For C2, let $x$ and $y$ be two nodes in $N'$ such that $x <' y$; the only interesting case is when one of the nodes is $u$. The proof uses the transitivity of $R_c$.

**S3-defects (P-case).** Assume there is a node $s$ in $N$ and a formula $P\psi \in \lambda(s)$ for which there is no $t$ in $N$ such that $t < s$ and $\psi \in \lambda(t)$.

Let $m$ be the unique point in $N$ such that (1) $(m, P\psi)$ is an S3-defect in $N$, and (2) for all $w < m$, $(w, P\psi)$ is not a defect. Such an $m$ must exist.

Choose some new point $s' \notin S$ and let $\Sigma'$ be an MCS containing $\psi$ such that $\Sigma' R_c \lambda(m)$; such a $\Sigma'$ exists by the Existence Lemma for normal logics. Define $N' = (N', <', \lambda')$ as follows:

$$N' := N \cup \{s'\}; \quad <' \;:=\; < \;\cup\; \{(x, s') \mid x < m\} \cup \{(s', x) \mid m \leq x\}; \quad \lambda' := \lambda \cup \{(s', \Sigma')\}.$$

Observe that $F_{N'}$ is a strict total order, and that $N'$ does not contain the defect $(s, P\psi)$. It remains to check coherence. Consider $x, y \in N'$ with $x <' y$. If $y = s'$, by construction $\lambda(s') = \Sigma' R_c \lambda(m)$, and by coherency of $N$, $\lambda(x) R_c \lambda(m)$. But $R_c$ is the canonical relation for $\mathbf{K_tQ}$ --- a relation with no branching to the left --- hence either $\Sigma' R_c \lambda(x)$, $\Sigma' = \lambda(x)$, or $\lambda(x) R_c \Sigma'$. The first two options lead to contradictions with the minimality of $m$. We conclude that $\lambda(x) R_c \Sigma'$, establishing coherence. $\square$

### Main Completeness Theorem

**Theorem 4.65.** $\mathbf{K_tQ}$ is strongly complete with respect to $(\mathbb{Q}, <)$.

*Proof.* Choose some set $S = \{s_i \mid i \in \omega\}$ and enumerate the set of potential defects (the union of $S$, $S \times S$ and $S \times \{F, P\} \times \mathrm{Form}$). Given a consistent set of formulas $\Sigma$, expand it to an MCS $\Gamma_0$. Let $N_0$ be the network $(\{s_0\}, \varnothing, (s_0, \Gamma_0))$. Trivially, $N_0$ is a finite, coherent network for $\Gamma_0$.

Let $n \geq 0$ and suppose $N_n$ is a finite, coherent network. Let $D$ be the defect of $N_n$ that is minimal in our enumeration. Form $N_{n+1}$ by repairing the defect $D$ as described in the proof of the Repair Lemma. Observe that $D$ will not be a defect of any network extending $N_n$.

Let $N = (N, <, \lambda)$ be given by $N = \bigcup_{n \in \omega} N_n$, $< = \bigcup_{n \in \omega} <_n$, and $\lambda = \bigcup_{n \in \omega} \lambda_n$.

It is easy to see that $F_N$ is a strict total order. Moreover, as we chose the points in $N$ from a countably infinite set, $N$ is countable.

$N$ is perfect: suppose $N$ is not perfect; let $D$ be the minimal defect, say $D = D_k$. By construction, there must be an approximation $N_i$ of which $D$ is also a defect. $D$ need not be the minimal defect of $N_i$, but there can be at most $k$ defects that are more urgent, so $D$ will be repaired before stage $k + i$ of the construction.

Finally, by the perfection of $N$ it follows from Lemma 4.61 that the induced model $I_N$ satisfies $\Gamma$ at $s_0$. $\square$

## 4.7 Rules for the Undefinable

*(Book pp. 231--241)*

In the previous two sections we proved semantically driven completeness results by using standard canonical models indirectly. The present section takes a rather different approach: we enrich the deductive system with a special proof rule, and consider a special (not necessarily generated) submodel of the canonical model for this new logic. The submodel contains only special *distinguishing* (or *witnessing*) MCSs. The completeness proof shows that this new canonical model has all the good properties of the original, and that, in addition, it is already in the right shape.

The running example is (again) the tense logic of dense unbounded strict total orderings. The difficulty when working with this logic is that there is no axiom ensuring the irreflexivity of the canonical frame. In previous sections we achieved irreflexivity indirectly. In this section we will construct a canonical frame that is transitive, non-branching, dense and irreflexive right from the start.

### The IRR Rule

The starting point is that irreflexivity, although not definable in basic modal languages, can be characterized in an alternative sense:

> If a temporal formula $\varphi$ is satisfiable on an irreflexive frame, then for any proposition letter $p$ not occurring in $\varphi$, the conjunction $(\neg Pp \land p \land \neg Fp) \land \varphi$ is also satisfiable on that frame.

For, if $F, V, s \Vdash \varphi$, then $F, V', s \Vdash (\neg Pp \land p \land \neg Fp) \land \varphi$, where $V'$ is just like $V$ except that it assigns the singleton $\{s\}$ to $p$.

By taking the contrapositive, we turn this into a proof rule:

$$\textbf{(IRR)}\quad \text{if } \vdash (\neg Pp \land p \land \neg Fp) \to \varphi \text{ then } \vdash \varphi, \text{ provided } p \text{ does not occur in } \varphi.$$

This rule is sound on the class of irreflexive frames. On strict total orders the formula $(\neg P\alpha \land \alpha \land \neg F\alpha)$ is true at some state $s$ iff $s$ is the only state where $\alpha$ holds. That is, $\neg P\alpha \land \alpha \land \neg F\alpha$ acts as a sort of "name" for the satisfying point. Call this formula $\mathrm{name}(\alpha)$.

### The Logic $\mathbf{K_tQ^+}$

**Definition 4.66.** The logic $\mathbf{K_tQ^+}$ is obtained by adding to $\mathbf{K_tQ}$ the irreflexivity rule IRR. In what follows, consistency means $\mathbf{K_tQ^+}$-consistency, $\vdash \varphi$ means that $\varphi$ is provable in $\mathbf{K_tQ^+}$, and so on. The canonical model for $\mathbf{K_tQ^+}$ is denoted by $M_c$, the canonical relation by $R_c$.

### Witnessing MCSs

**Definition 4.67.** A maximal consistent set is called *witnessing* if it contains a formula of the form $\mathrm{name}(\alpha)$.

**Why witnessing MCSs are interesting:** Suppose we deal with a collection $W$ of witnessing maximal consistent sets. This collection induces a model in the obvious way. If we can prove a Truth Lemma, then it is immediate that the underlying relation of the model is irreflexive: $\mathrm{name}(\alpha) \in \Gamma$ implies $\alpha \in \Gamma$ and $F\alpha \notin \Gamma$.

There are two issues with throwing away non-witnessing MCSs:

(i) **The Existence Lemma**: whenever $F\psi$ is in some witnessing MCS $\Gamma$, there must be a witnessing $\Delta$ such that $\Gamma R_c \Delta$ and $\psi \in \Delta$. The Existence Lemma requires stronger saturation: whenever $F\varphi \in \Gamma$, then there is some $\alpha$ such that $F(\varphi \land \mathrm{name}(\alpha)) \in \Gamma$ too.

(ii) **Existential axiom properties**: For axioms with existential import (e.g., the density axiom), how can we ensure the trimmed canonical model still validates these properties?

### Diamond saturation

**Definition 4.68.** Let $W$ be a set of maximal consistent sets of formulas. Define $M_c|_W$ to be the submodel of the canonical model induced by $W$; that is, $M_c|_W = (W, R, V)$ where $R$ is $R_c$ restricted to $W$, and $V$ is the canonical valuation restricted to $W$.

**Definition 4.69.** A set $W$ of maximal consistent sets is called *diamond saturated* if for each $\Gamma \in W$ and each formula $F\psi \in \Gamma$ there is a set $\Delta \in W$ such that $\Gamma R_c \Delta$ and $\psi \in \Delta$, and the analogous condition holds for past formulas.

**Lemma 4.70 (Truth Lemma).** Let $W$ be a diamond saturated set of maximal consistent sets of formulas. Then for any $\Gamma \in W$ and any formula $\varphi$:

$$M_c|_W, \Gamma \Vdash \varphi \quad\text{iff}\quad \varphi \in \Gamma.$$

*Proof.* Straightforward by induction on $\varphi$. $\square$

### Construction of witnessing diamond-saturated sets

**Proposition 4.71.** Let $\varphi$ be some consistent formula. Then there is a countable, diamond saturated collection $W$ of witnessing MCSs such that $\varphi \in \Gamma$ for some $\Gamma \in W$.

*Proof (sketch).* The basic idea is to define $W$ step-by-step, in a sort of parallel Lindenbaum construction on graphs. Approximations to $W$ are called *networks*: a network is a quadruple $N = (N, E, d, \lambda)$ such that $(N, E)$ is a finite, undirected, connected and acyclic graph; $d$ is a direction function mapping each edge $(s, t)$ to either $R$ or its converse $R^{-1}$; and $\lambda$ is a label function mapping each node to a finite set of formulas.

For nodes $s, t$ adjacent in $N$, define:

$$\langle st \rangle := \begin{cases} F & \text{if } d(s,t) = R \\ P & \text{if } d(t,s) = R \end{cases}$$

Let $E(s)$ denote the set of nodes adjacent to $s$, and $\sigma(s)$ denote $\bigwedge \lambda(s)$. Define:

$$\chi(N, s) := \sigma(s) \land \bigwedge_{v \in E(s)} \langle sv \rangle \chi'(N, v, s)$$

$$\chi'(N, t, s) := \sigma(t) \land \bigwedge_{s \neq v \in E(s)} \langle tv \rangle \chi'(N, v, t)$$

**Lemma 4.72.** For any network $N$ and any two nodes $s, t$ in $N$, $\chi(N, s)$ is consistent iff $\chi(N, t)$ is consistent.

*Proof.* It suffices to prove for adjacent $s$ and $t$; assume $d(s,t) = R$. Then:

$$\chi(s) = F\chi'(t,s) \land \chi'(s,t) \quad\text{and}\quad \chi(t) = \chi'(t,s) \land P\chi'(s,t).$$

But $F\alpha \land \beta$ is consistent iff $\alpha \land P\beta$ is consistent in any logic extending $\mathbf{K_t}$. $\square$

We call $N$ *coherent* if $\chi(N, s)$ is consistent for some (equivalently: all) nodes $s$.

**Defects:** (D1) a pair $(s, \varphi)$ such that neither $\varphi$ nor $\neg\varphi$ belongs to $\lambda(s)$; (D2) a pair $(s, F\varphi)$ such that $F\varphi \in \lambda(s)$ while there is no witness; (D3) similar for $P\varphi$; (D4) a node $s$ without a name.

**Lemma 4.73.** For any defect of a finite, coherent network $N$ there is a finite, coherent $N' \supseteq N$ lacking this defect.

*Proof (sketch).*

- **D1-defects:** Since $\chi(N, s)$ is consistent, either $\chi(N, s) \land \varphi$ or $\chi(N, s) \land \neg\varphi$ is consistent; add the consistent one to $\lambda(s)$.

- **D2-defects:** Take a new node $t$, add edge $(s, t)$ with $d(s,t) = R$, and set $\lambda'(t) = \{\varphi\}$. Since $F\varphi$ was already a conjunct of $\sigma(s)$, the description formula $\chi(N', s) = \chi(N, s)$.

- **D3-defects:** Analogous to D2.

- **D4-defects:** Repaired using the IRR-rule: if $\chi(N, s)$ is consistent, then there is a propositional variable $p$ not occurring in any label set, and $\chi(N, s) \land \mathrm{name}(p)$ is consistent.

**Completion of Proposition 4.71:** By a standard step-by-step construction, define a sequence $(N_i)_{i \in \mathbb{N}}$ of networks such that: (i) $N_0$ is a one-node network with label set $\{\varphi\}$, (ii) $N_j$ extends $N_i$ whenever $i < j$, (iii) for every defect of any $N_i$ there is a $N_j$ with $j > i$ lacking this defect.

Let $N = \bigcup_{i \in \mathbb{N}} N_i$; for $s \in N$, define $\lambda(s) = \bigcup_{i \in \mathbb{N}} \lambda_i(s)$. Then each $\lambda(s)$ is a witnessing MCS, and $W = \mathrm{range}(\lambda)$ is diamond saturated. $\square$

### Density of the submodel

**Lemma 4.74.** Let $W$ be a diamond saturated collection of witnessing maximal consistent sets, and let $<$ denote $R_c$ restricted to $W$. Then $(W, <)$ is a non-branching, unbounded, dense, strict ordering.

*Proof.* $(W, <)$ is a subframe of the canonical frame; hence it inherits every universal property of $T$, such as transitivity or non-branching. Irreflexivity follows from the fact that $\Gamma R_c \Gamma$ for no witnessing $\Gamma$.

Unboundedness follows from $F\top$ and $P\top$ being theorems.

For density: assume $\Gamma < \Delta$. Let $\alpha$ be such that $\mathrm{name}(\alpha) \in \Delta$. Then $F\mathrm{name}(\alpha) \in \Gamma$, so by the density axiom, $FF\mathrm{name}(\alpha) \in \Gamma$. From this we infer the existence of $\Sigma \in W$ with $\Gamma < \Sigma$ and $F\mathrm{name}(\alpha) \in \Sigma$.

Is $\Sigma < \Delta$? Since $<$ is non-branching to the right, we know $\Sigma < \Delta$ or $\Sigma = \Delta$ or $\Delta < \Sigma$. But $\Sigma = \Delta$ is impossible since $F\alpha \in \Sigma$ and $\neg F\alpha \in \Delta$. Neither is $\Delta < \Sigma$ possible, for $F\alpha \in \Sigma$ would imply $FF\alpha \in \Delta$, hence $F\alpha \in \Delta$ by transitivity, contradicting $\neg F\alpha \in \Delta$. $\square$

### Main Theorem

**Theorem 4.75.** $\mathbf{K_tQ^+}$ is complete with respect to $(\mathbb{Q}, <)$.

*Proof.* Given any consistent formula $\varphi$, construct a countable, diamond saturated set $W$ of witnessing MCSs for $\varphi$, as in Proposition 4.71. By Truth Lemma 4.70, $\varphi$ is satisfiable at some MCS $\Gamma$ in $M_c|_W$; and by Lemma 4.74, this model is based on a non-branching, unbounded, dense, strict ordering. The subframe generated by $\Gamma$ is based on a countable, dense, unbounded, strict total order, hence isomorphic to $(\mathbb{Q}, <)$. $\square$

### Applicability and limitations

- The method only works well in **tense logic** (operators for both directions). In purely modal (one-direction) settings, one needs a countably infinite family of increasingly complex rules instead of a single IRR rule.
- Only **weak completeness** was proved for $\mathbf{K_tQ^+}$, because the proof of Lemma 4.71 uses finite networks. In the presence of names, however, a stronger version is possible (Exercise 4.7.2).
- The **inheritance problem**: which properties of the canonical frame survive in a subframe? Universal properties survive, as do first-order properties that are standard translations of closed modal formulas. Names are indispensable for proving inheritance of universal-existential properties like density.

### Named models and Sahlqvist completeness

**Definition 4.76.** Let $\tau$ be some modal similarity type. A $\tau$-model $M$ is called *named* if for every state $s$ in $M$ there is a formula $\varphi$ such that $s$ is the only point in $M$ satisfying $\varphi$.

**Theorem 4.77.** Let $\tau$ be some modal similarity type, and suppose $M = (F, V)$ is a named $\tau$-model. Then for every very simple Sahlqvist formula $\varphi$:

$$M \vDash \varphi \quad\text{iff}\quad F \vDash \varphi. \tag{4.1}$$

If, in addition, $M$ is a versatile model for $\tau$, then (4.1) holds for every Sahlqvist formula.

*Proof.* Let $M$ be a named model. The collection $A := \{V(\varphi) \mid \varphi \text{ a formula}\}$ is closed under the boolean and modal operations. Hence, the structure $\mathfrak{g} = (F, A)$ is a general frame. Since $M$ is named, $A$ contains all singletons. The result follows from Theorem 5.90 in Chapter 5. $\square$

## 4.8 Finitary Methods I

*(Book pp. 241--248)*

In this section we introduce **finite canonical models**. We use such models to prove weak completeness results for non-compact logics. We examine one of the best known examples --- **propositional dynamic logic (PDL)** --- in detail.

Recall that PDL has a set of diamonds $\langle\pi\rangle$ indexed by a collection of programs $\Pi$. $\Pi$ consists of a collection of basic programs, and the programs generated from them using the constructors $\cup$, $;$, and ${}^*$. A frame for this language is a transition system $F = (W, R_\pi)_{\pi \in \Pi}$, but we are only interested in *regular* frames:

$$R_{\pi_1 \cup \pi_2} = R_{\pi_1} \cup R_{\pi_2}, \quad R_{\pi_1;\pi_2} = R_{\pi_1} ; R_{\pi_2}, \quad R_{\pi^*} = (R_\pi)^*.$$

The collection of PDL-validities is **not compact**: the set $\Sigma = \{\langle a^*\rangle p, \neg p, \neg\langle a\rangle p, \neg\langle a\rangle\langle a\rangle p, \ldots\}$ is finitely satisfiable on regular frames but not itself satisfiable.

### The axiom system PDL

**Definition 4.78.** A logic $\Lambda$ in the language of propositional dynamic logic is a *normal propositional dynamic logic* if it contains every instance of the following axiom schemas:

(i) $[\pi]( p \to q) \to ([\pi]p \to [\pi]q)$

(ii) $\langle\pi\rangle p \leftrightarrow \neg[\pi]\neg p$

(iii) $\langle\pi_1;\pi_2\rangle p \leftrightarrow \langle\pi_1\rangle\langle\pi_2\rangle p$

(iv) $\langle\pi_1 \cup \pi_2\rangle p \leftrightarrow \langle\pi_1\rangle p \lor \langle\pi_2\rangle p$

(v) $\langle\pi^*\rangle p \leftrightarrow (p \lor \langle\pi\rangle\langle\pi^*\rangle p)$

(vi) $[\pi^*](p \to [\pi]p) \to (p \to [\pi^*]p)$ &emsp; *(induction axiom)*

and is closed under modus ponens, generalization ($\vdash \varphi$ implies $\vdash [\pi]\varphi$ for all programs $\pi$) and uniform substitution. We call the smallest such logic **PDL**.

### Fischer-Ladner closure

**Definition 4.79 (Fischer-Ladner Closure).** Let $X$ be a set of formulas. Then $X$ is *Fischer-Ladner closed* if it is closed under subformulas and:

(i) If $\langle\pi_1;\pi_2\rangle\varphi \in X$ then $\langle\pi_1\rangle\langle\pi_2\rangle\varphi \in X$

(ii) If $\langle\pi_1 \cup \pi_2\rangle\varphi \in X$ then $\langle\pi_1\rangle\varphi \lor \langle\pi_2\rangle\varphi \in X$

(iii) If $\langle\pi^*\rangle\varphi \in X$ then $\langle\pi\rangle\langle\pi^*\rangle\varphi \in X$.

If $\Phi$ is any set of formulas then $\mathrm{FL}(\Phi)$ is the smallest Fischer-Ladner closed set containing $\Phi$.

Given a formula $\varphi$, define $\widetilde{\varphi} := \psi$ if $\varphi = \neg\psi$; $\widetilde{\varphi} := \neg\varphi$ otherwise. A set $X$ is *closed under single negations* if $\widetilde{\varphi} \in X$ whenever $\varphi \in X$. We define $\overline{\mathrm{FL}}(\Phi)$, the *closure* of $\Phi$, as the smallest set containing $\Phi$ which is Fischer-Ladner closed and closed under single negations.

If $\Phi$ is finite, then so is its closure.

### Atoms

**Definition 4.80 (Atoms).** Let $\Phi$ be a set of formulas. A set of formulas $A$ is an *atom* over $\Phi$ if it is a maximal consistent subset of $\overline{\mathrm{FL}}(\Phi)$. $\mathrm{At}(\Phi)$ is the set of all atoms over $\Phi$.

**Lemma 4.81.** Let $\Phi$ be any set of formulas, and $A \in \mathrm{At}(\Phi)$. Then:

(i) For all $\varphi \in \overline{\mathrm{FL}}(\Phi)$: exactly one of $\varphi$ and $\widetilde{\varphi}$ is in $A$.

(ii) For all $\varphi \lor \psi \in \overline{\mathrm{FL}}(\Phi)$: $\varphi \lor \psi \in A$ iff $\varphi \in A$ or $\psi \in A$.

(iii) For all $\langle\pi_1;\pi_2\rangle\varphi \in \overline{\mathrm{FL}}(\Phi)$: $\langle\pi_1;\pi_2\rangle\varphi \in A$ iff $\langle\pi_1\rangle\langle\pi_2\rangle\varphi \in A$.

(iv) For all $\langle\pi_1 \cup \pi_2\rangle\varphi \in \overline{\mathrm{FL}}(\Phi)$: $\langle\pi_1 \cup \pi_2\rangle\varphi \in A$ iff $\langle\pi_1\rangle\varphi \in A$ or $\langle\pi_2\rangle\varphi \in A$.

(v) For all $\langle\pi^*\rangle\varphi \in \overline{\mathrm{FL}}(\Phi)$: $\langle\pi^*\rangle\varphi \in A$ iff $\varphi \in A$ or $\langle\pi\rangle\langle\pi^*\rangle\varphi \in A$.

**Lemma 4.82.** Let $\mathcal{M}$ be the set of all MCSs, and $\Phi$ any set of formulas. Then $\mathrm{At}(\Phi) = \{\Gamma \cap \overline{\mathrm{FL}}(\Phi) \mid \Gamma \in \mathcal{M}\}$.

**Lemma 4.83.** If $\varphi \in \overline{\mathrm{FL}}(\Phi)$ and $\varphi$ is consistent, then there is an $A \in \mathrm{At}(\Phi)$ such that $\varphi \in A$.

*Proof.* Enumerate $\overline{\mathrm{FL}}(\Phi)$ as $\varphi_1, \ldots, \varphi_m$. Let $A_1 = \{\varphi_1\}$. Given $A_n$ (where $n < m$), since $\vdash \widehat{A_n} \leftrightarrow (\widehat{A_n} \land \varphi_{n+1}) \lor (\widehat{A_n} \land \widetilde{\varphi_{n+1}})$, either $A_n \cup \{\varphi_{n+1}\}$ or $A_n \cup \{\widetilde{\varphi_{n+1}}\}$ is consistent. Let $A_{n+1}$ be the consistent extension, and $A = A_m$. $\square$

### Finite canonical models

**Definition 4.84 (Canonical Model over $\Phi$).** Let $\Phi$ be a finite set of formulas. The *canonical model over $\Phi$* is the triple $(\mathrm{At}(\Phi), \{S_\pi^\Phi\}_{\pi \in \Pi}, V^\Phi)$ where for all propositional variables $p$, $V^\Phi(p) = \{A \in \mathrm{At}(\Phi) \mid p \in A\}$, and for all atoms $A, B \in \mathrm{At}(\Phi)$ and all programs $\pi$: $AS_\pi^\Phi B$ if $\widehat{A} \land \langle\pi\rangle\widehat{B}$ is consistent.

**Definition 4.85 (Regular PDL-model over $\Phi$).** For all basic programs $a$, define $R_a^\Phi = S_a^\Phi$. For complex programs, define the PDL-relations $R_\pi^\Phi$ inductively using unions, compositions, and reflexive transitive closures. The *regular PDL-model over $\Phi$* is $\mathcal{R} = (\mathrm{At}(\Phi), \{R_\pi^\Phi\}_{\pi \in \Pi}, V^\Phi)$.

### Key lemma: the induction axiom at work

**Lemma 4.86 (Existence Lemma for Basic Programs).** Let $A$ be an atom, and $a$ a basic program. Then for all formulas $\langle a\rangle\psi$ in $\overline{\mathrm{FL}}(\Phi)$: $\langle a\rangle\psi \in A$ iff there is a $B \in \mathrm{At}(\Phi)$ such that $AR_aB$ and $\psi \in B$.

*Proof.* The right-to-left direction uses the definition of the canonical relation. For left-to-right, suppose $\langle a\rangle\psi \in A$. We construct an appropriate atom $B$ by "forcing choices": enumerate $\overline{\mathrm{FL}}(\Phi)$ as $\varphi_1, \ldots, \varphi_m$. Define $B_0 = \{\psi\}$. Given $B_n$, since $\vdash \langle a\rangle\widehat{B_n} \leftrightarrow (\langle a\rangle(\widehat{B_n} \land \varphi_{n+1}) \lor \langle a\rangle(\widehat{B_n} \land \widetilde{\varphi_{n+1}}))$, one of the two extensions keeps $\widehat{A} \land \langle a\rangle\widehat{B'}$ consistent. $\square$

**Lemma 4.87.** For all programs $\pi$, $S_{\pi^*} \subseteq (S_\pi)^*$.

*Proof.* We need to show that if $AS_{\pi^*}B$ then there is a finite sequence of atoms $C_0, \ldots, C_n$ such that $A = C_0 S_\pi C_1, \ldots, C_{n-1} S_\pi C_n = B$. Let $D$ be the set of all atoms reachable from $A$ by such a sequence. Define $\delta = \bigvee_{D \in D} \widehat{D}$.

Note that $\delta \land \langle\pi\rangle\neg\delta$ is inconsistent (otherwise some atom not in $D$ would be reachable). Hence $\vdash \delta \to [\pi]\delta$, so by generalization $\vdash [\pi^*](\delta \to [\pi]\delta)$. By axiom (vi), $\vdash \delta \to [\pi^*]\delta$.

Since $AS_{\pi^*}A$, $\widehat{A}$ is a disjunct in $\delta$, so $\vdash \widehat{A} \to [\pi^*]\delta$. As $\widehat{A} \land \langle\pi^*\rangle\widehat{B}$ is consistent, $\widehat{A} \land \langle\pi^*\rangle(\widehat{B} \land \delta)$ is consistent too. This means $\widehat{B} \land \widehat{D}$ is consistent for some $D \in D$. As atoms, $B = D$, hence $B \in D$. $\square$

**Lemma 4.88.** For all programs $\pi$, $S_\pi \subseteq R_\pi$.

*Proof.* Induction on the structure of $\pi$. The base case is immediate. For $\pi_1;\pi_2$: if $AS_{\pi_1;\pi_2}B$, then by axiom (iii), $\widehat{A} \land \langle\pi_1\rangle\langle\pi_2\rangle\widehat{B}$ is consistent. By forcing choices construct atom $C$ such that both $\widehat{A} \land \langle\pi_1\rangle\widehat{C}$ and $\widehat{C} \land \langle\pi_2\rangle\widehat{B}$ are consistent. By inductive hypothesis, $AR_{\pi_1}C$ and $CR_{\pi_2}B$, so $AR_{\pi_1;\pi_2}B$. Similarly for $\cup$. The case for $\pi^*$ follows from Lemma 4.87 and $S_\pi \subseteq R_\pi$ implies $(S_\pi)^* \subseteq (R_\pi)^*$. $\square$

**Lemma 4.89 (Existence Lemma).** Let $A$ be an atom and let $\langle\pi\rangle\psi$ be a formula in $\overline{\mathrm{FL}}(\Phi)$. Then $\langle\pi\rangle\psi \in A$ iff there is a $B$ such that $AR_\pi B$ and $\psi \in B$.

*Proof.* Left-to-right: suppose $\langle\pi\rangle\psi \in A$. Build atom $B$ with $AS_\pi B$ by forcing choices. Since $S_\pi \subseteq R_\pi$, $AR_\pi B$ as well.

Right-to-left: by induction on the structure of $\pi$. The base case is the Existence Lemma for basic programs. For $\pi_1;\pi_2$: if $AR_{\pi_1;\pi_2}B$ and $\psi \in B$, there is atom $C$ with $AR_{\pi_1}C$ and $CR_{\pi_2}B$. By inductive hypothesis, $\langle\pi_2\rangle\psi \in C$, then $\langle\pi_1\rangle\langle\pi_2\rangle\psi \in A$, hence $\langle\pi_1;\pi_2\rangle\psi \in A$.

For $\pi^*$: if $AR_{\pi^*}B$ and $\psi \in B$, there is a sequence $A = C_0 R_\pi C_1, \ldots, C_{n-1} R_\pi C_n = B$. By subinduction on $n$ we prove $\langle\pi^*\rangle\psi \in C_i$ for all $i$. Base: $n=0$, so $A = B$ and $\vdash \psi \to \langle\pi^*\rangle\psi$ by axiom (v). Inductive step: $\langle\pi^*\rangle\psi \in C_1$ by hypothesis, hence $\langle\pi\rangle\langle\pi^*\rangle\psi \in A$, hence $\langle\pi^*\rangle\psi \in A$ by axiom (v). $\square$

**Lemma 4.90 (Truth Lemma).** Let $\mathcal{R}$ be the regular PDL-model over $\Phi$. For all atoms $A$ and all $\psi \in \overline{\mathrm{FL}}(\Phi)$: $\mathcal{R}, A \Vdash \psi$ iff $\psi \in A$.

**Theorem 4.91.** PDL is weakly complete with respect to the class of all regular frames.

## 4.9 Finitary Methods II

*(Book pp. 248--258)*

All normal logics extending $\mathbf{S4.3}$ are particularly well-behaved. We prove three results about them:

1. **Bull's Theorem:** all such logics have the finite frame property.
2. **Finite axiomatizability:** they are all finitely axiomatizable.
3. **Negative characterization:** each has a negative characterization in terms of finite sets of finite frames.

The logics extending $\mathbf{S4.3}$ are logics of frames that are rooted, transitive, and connected ($\forall xy(Rxy \lor Ryx)$). Any S4.3 frame can be viewed as a chain of clusters.

### Bull's Theorem

**Goal:** Show that if $\Lambda$ extends $\mathbf{S4.3}$, then any $\Lambda$-consistent formula $\varphi$ is satisfiable in a finite model $(W, R, V)$ such that $(W, R) \vDash \Lambda$.

**Method:** We transform the canonical model. Given $\Lambda$-consistent $\varphi$:

- Let $w$ be a $\Lambda$-MCS containing $\varphi$, and $M_w = (W_w, R_w, V_w)$ the submodel of $M_\Lambda$ generated by $w$. Then $M_w, w \Vdash \varphi$ and $M_w$ is based on an S4.3 frame.
- Transform $M_w$ into a finite model $M_s$ satisfying $\varphi$ and based on an S4.3 frame that validates $\Lambda$.

**Step 1: Filtration.** Let $\Sigma$ be the finite set of all subformulas of $\varphi$ preceded by $\Diamond$, and let $M_f = (W_f, R_f, V_f)$ be the result of transitively filtrating $M_w$ through $\Sigma$. Then $M_f$ is finite, based on an S4.3 frame (a finite chain of finite clusters).

**Step 2: Elimination.** We want to remove all points in $M_f$ that prevent the natural map from being a bounded morphism.

**Definition 4.92.** Suppose $\alpha, \beta \in W_f$. We say that $\beta$ is *subordinate* to $\alpha$ ($\beta \operatorname{sub} \alpha$) if there is a $v \in \alpha$ such that for all $z \in \beta$, it is not the case that $R_w vz$.

**Lemma 4.93.**

(i) If $\beta \operatorname{sub} \alpha$, then there is a $v \in \alpha$ such that for all $z \in \beta$, $R_w zv$.

(ii) If $\beta \operatorname{sub} \alpha$ then $R_f \alpha \beta$.

(iii) The sub relation is transitive and asymmetric.

(iv) If $\alpha \operatorname{sub} \beta$ and not $\gamma \operatorname{sub} \beta$, then $\alpha \operatorname{sub} \gamma$.

**Lemma 4.94 (Existence Lemma).** Let $u \in W_w$ and suppose $\Diamond\psi \in u \cap \Sigma$. Then there is a $|v| \in W_f$ such that $R_f|u||v|$, $\psi \in |v|$, and $|v|$ is not eliminable.

*Proof (sketch).* Construct a maximal sequence $\alpha_0, \alpha_1, \ldots$ through $W_f$ with: (i) $\alpha_0 = |u|$; (ii) if $i > 0$ and odd, then $\alpha_i = |v|$ such that $\psi \in v$, $R_f\alpha_{i-1}|v|$, and not $|v| \operatorname{sub} \alpha_{i-1}$; (iii) if $i > 0$ and even, then $\alpha_i = |v|$ such that $R_f|v|\alpha_{i-1}$ and $\alpha_{i-1} \operatorname{sub} |v|$.

- *Claim 1:* For every $\alpha_i = |v|$, $\Diamond\psi \in v$.
- *Claim 2:* The sequence terminates (by transitivity and asymmetry of sub).
- *Claim 3:* The sequence does not terminate on even $i$.

Hence the sequence terminates on odd $\alpha_m = |v|$ with $\psi \in v$, $|v|$ not eliminable, and $R_f|u||v|$ by transitivity. $\square$

**Model $M_s$:** Let $W_s$ be the set of non-eliminable points in $W_f$. Then $M_s = (W_s, R_s, V_s)$ is $M_f$ restricted to $W_s$. Hence $M_s$ is a finite distinguishing model based on an S4.3 frame.

**Lemma 4.95.** $M_s$ satisfies $\varphi$.

*Proof.* By induction on structure of $\psi$ (for $\psi \in \Sigma$) and all $|u| \in W_s$: $M_s, |u| \Vdash \psi$ iff $\psi \in u$. The modal case uses Lemma 4.94. $\square$

**Theorem 4.96 (Bull's Theorem).** Every normal modal logic extending $\mathbf{S4.3}$ has the finite frame property.

*Proof.* Define $f : W_w \to W_s$ by $f(w) = |w|$ if $|w| \in W_s$; otherwise, the first $R_s$-minimal element of $\{\sigma \in W_s \mid R_s|w|\sigma\}$.

Define $V'(p) = \{u \in W_w \mid f(u) \in V_s(p)\}$ and $M' = (W_w, R_w, V')$. Then $M'$ is a definable variant of $M_w$, and $f$ is a surjective bounded morphism from $M'$ to $M_s$.

Following the upper path in Figure 4.2: $M_\Lambda \vDash \Lambda$, hence $M_w \vDash \Lambda$ (generated submodel). As $M'$ is a definable variant, $M' \vDash \Lambda$ by Lemma 3.25. As $M_s$ is a bounded morphic image of $M'$, $M_s \vDash \Lambda$. Since $M_s$ is finite and distinguishing, its frame validates $\Lambda$. $\square$

### Finite axiomatizability

The proof uses a representation of finite S4.3 frames as *lists of positive integers* (each integer records the cardinality of the corresponding cluster).

**Definition 4.97.** A *list* is a finite non-empty list of positive integers. A list $\mathbf{t}$ *contains* a list $\mathbf{s}$ if $\mathbf{t}$ has a sublist of the same length as $\mathbf{s}$, each item $\geq$ the corresponding item of $\mathbf{s}$. A list $\mathbf{t}$ *covers* a list $\mathbf{s}$ if $\mathbf{t}$ contains $\mathbf{s}$ and the last item of $\mathbf{t}$ is $\geq$ the last item of $\mathbf{s}$.

**Lemma 4.98.** Let $F$ and $G$ be finite S4.3 frames with associated lists $\mathbf{f}$ and $\mathbf{g}$. Then $\mathbf{f}$ covers $\mathbf{g}$ iff there is a bounded morphism from $F$ onto $G$.

**Theorem 4.99 (Kruskal's Theorem).** Every countably infinite sequence of lists $\mathbf{t}$ contains an infinite subsequence $\mathbf{s}$ such that for all $\mathbf{s}_i$ and $\mathbf{s}_j$ in $\mathbf{s}$, $i > j$ implies $\mathbf{s}_i$ covers $\mathbf{s}_j$.

*Proof (sketch).* Show every infinite sequence of lists contains a chain of length 2. The argument proceeds by cases: handle one-item lists using monotone subsequences of natural numbers; for longer lists, use a minimality argument decomposing into heads and tails, and apply (4.3) to heads and induction to tails. Then iterate to find an infinite chain. $\square$

**Corollary 4.100.** There is no infinite sequence $\Lambda_0, \Lambda_1, \ldots$ of normal logics containing $\mathbf{S4.3}$ such that for all $i$, $\Lambda_i \subsetneq \Lambda_{i+1}$.

*Proof.* If such a sequence existed, for each $i$ there is $\varphi_i \in \Lambda_{i+1} \setminus \Lambda_i$. By Bull's Theorem, there is a finite S4.3 frame $F_i$ validating $\Lambda_i$ but not $\varphi_i$. By Kruskal's Theorem, there exist $k > l$ such that $\mathbf{t}_k$ covers $\mathbf{t}_l$. By Lemma 4.98, there is a bounded morphism from $F_k$ onto $F_l$. But then $F_l \vDash \Lambda_l$ --- contradiction. $\square$

**Theorem 4.101.** Every normal modal logic extending $\mathbf{S4.3}$ is finitely axiomatizable.

*Proof.* Assume $\Lambda$ extends $\mathbf{S4.3}$ and is not finitely axiomatizable. Then $\Lambda$ is a proper extension of $\mathbf{S4.3}$. Let $\varphi_0 \in \Lambda \setminus \mathbf{S4.3}$, and $\Lambda_0$ the logic generated by $\mathbf{S4.3} \cup \{\varphi_0\}$. Then $\mathbf{S4.3} \subsetneq \Lambda_0 \subsetneq \Lambda$. Continuing, we find an infinite strictly ascending chain --- contradicting Corollary 4.100. $\square$

### Negative characterization

**Lemma 4.102.** All flat sets (sets of lists where no list covers another) are finite. For any set of lists $Y$ there is a maximal flat $X \subseteq Y$.

**Theorem 4.103.** For every normal modal logic $\Lambda$ extending $\mathbf{S4.3}$ there is a finite set $\mathcal{N}$ of finite S4.3 frames with the following property: for any finite frame $F$,

$$F \vDash \Lambda \quad\text{iff}\quad F \text{ is an S4.3 frame and there does not exist a bounded morphism from } F \text{ onto any frame in } \mathcal{N}.$$

*Proof.* Let $L_0$ be the set of lists associated with finite S4.3 frames not validating $\Lambda$. Let $L$ be a maximal flat subset of $L_0$. We claim: for any finite S4.3 frame $F$, $F \vDash \Lambda$ iff $F \notin B(C(L))$, where $C(L)$ is the set of lists covered by some list in $L$, and $B(X)$ is the class of frames admitting a bounded morphism onto a frame with list in $X$.

For each $\mathbf{g} \in C(L)$, choose a frame with list $\mathbf{g}$; let $\mathcal{N}$ be the set of all choices. $\square$

## 4.10 Summary of Chapter 4

*(Book pp. 258--259)*

- **Completeness:** A logic $\Lambda$ is *weakly complete* with respect to a class of structures $\mathcal{S}$ if every formula valid on $\mathcal{S}$ is a $\Lambda$-theorem. It is *strongly complete* if whenever a set of premises entails a conclusion over $\mathcal{S}$, then the conclusion is $\Lambda$-deducible from the premises.

- **Canonical Models and Frames:** The most important model building technique is the canonical model construction. The points of the canonical frame are maximal consistent sets of formulas, and the relations and valuation are defined in terms of membership.

- **Canonicity:** Many formulas are canonical for a property $P$: they are valid on any frame with $P$, and when used as axioms, they guarantee $P$ for the canonical frame. This yields strong completeness relatively straightforwardly.

- **Sahlqvist's Completeness Theorem:** Sahlqvist formulas not only define first-order properties of frames, each is also canonical for the first-order property it defines. Strong completeness is automatic for any logic axiomatized by Sahlqvist formulas.

- **Limitative Results:** The canonical model method is not universal: there are weakly complete logics whose axioms are not valid on any canonical frame. Indeed, there are logics that are not sound and weakly complete with respect to any class of frames at all.

- **Unraveling and Bulldozing:** For properties without canonical formulas, transformation methods can reshape canonical models. Unraveling and bulldozing are two useful methods.

- **Step-by-step:** Instead of modifying canonical models directly, the step-by-step method builds models by selecting MCSs inductively, offering great control over the resulting model's properties.

- **Rules for the Undefinable:** By enriching the deductive machinery with special proof rules, it is sometimes possible to construct canonical models with desired properties from the start.

- **Finitary Methods:** For non-compact logics (e.g., PDL), only weak completeness results are possible. Finite canonical models (essentially filtrations of standard canonical models) are a natural tool.

- **Logics extending S4.3:** All logics extending S4.3 have the finite frame property, are finitely axiomatizable, and have negative characterizations in terms of finite sets of finite frames.

## Notes

*(Book pp. 259--262)*

Modal completeness results can be proved using a variety of methods. Kripke's original modal proof systems [290, 291] were tableaux systems; see Fitting [145] for modal tableaux methods. Fine [139] uses normal forms to prove completeness of the McKinsey axiom logic (which is not canonical; see Goldblatt [193]).

Most modal completeness theory revolves around canonical models. Pioneering papers include Makinson [314] and Cresswell [97]. The full power emerged in Lemmon and Scott [303], who stated and proved the Canonical Model Theorem and established many frame completeness results, including a general canonicity result for axioms $\Diamond^k\Box^j p \to \Box^m\Diamond^n p$ ($k, j, m, n \geq 0$).

**KL:** Segerberg [396] proved weak completeness of KL with respect to finite transitive trees (derivations of 4 in KL were independently found by De Jongh, Kripke, and Sambin). The finite canonical model argument follows Hughes and Cresswell [241, Theorem 8.4].

**Incompleteness:** The tense logic $\mathbf{K_tThoM}$ was the first known frame-incomplete logic (Thomason [427]). Shortly afterward, Fine [137] and Thomason [427] exhibited incomplete logics in the basic modal language. The simpler $\mathbf{KvB}$ is due to van Benthem [38]. Blok [64, 65] showed that incompleteness is the rule rather than the exception.

**Transformation methods:** Systematic use stems from Segerberg [396], who developed bulldozing and used it (with filtration and other transformations) to prove completeness for the tense logics of $(\mathbb{N}, <)$, $(\mathbb{Z}, <)$, $(\mathbb{Q}, <)$, $(\mathbb{R}, <)$ and their reflexive counterparts.

**Step-by-step:** Burgess is an influential source (e.g., [76] for Since-Until logic, [77] for the basic modal language). De Jongh and Veltman [255] popularized the method among Amsterdam logicians. Arrow Logic uses the method heavily, often combined with rules for the undefinable [326].

**Rules for the undefinable:** Gabbay [158] is one of the earliest papers. Gabbay and Hodkinson [164] show such rules take a simple form in the basic temporal language. For the D-operator, see de Rijke [104] and Venema [439]. For nominals, see Passy and Tinchev [362], Gargov and Goranko [171], Blackburn and Tzakova [61], and Blackburn [55].

**PDL:** The axiomatization is from Segerberg's 1977 abstract [400]. A gap in Segerberg's proof was fixed in [402]; proofs by Parikh [357] and Kozen-Parikh [279] appeared in the meantime. The text proof is based on van Benthem and Meyer Viol [48].

**Bull's Theorem and logics extending S4.3:** Bull's original proof [72] was algebraic; the model-theoretic proof is due to Fine [136]. See Bull and Segerberg [73] for a comparison. Goldblatt [183] presents Fine's proof emphasizing "clusters within clusters." Fine's paper also contains the finite axiomatizability result (Theorem 4.101) and the negative characterization (Theorem 4.103).

The work of Bull and Fine initiated investigation into subregions of the lattice of normal modal logics. See Maksimova [317], Esakia and Meskhi [132], Blok [65] for logics characterized by single structures. Fine [138, 141] analyzes logics extending K4.3 (technically demanding as not all have the finite frame property). The Berlin school: Rautenberg [374--376], Kracht [283, 285, 286], Wolter [452]. For tense logics: Kracht [281], Wolter [450, 451].

Zakharyaschev [462--465] has studied an alternative frame-theoretic approach: given a modal formula $\varphi$, one can effectively construct finite rooted frames $F_1, \ldots, F_n$ such that a general frame $\mathfrak{g}$ refutes $\varphi$ iff there is a subframe of $\mathfrak{g}$ satisfying natural conditions that maps to some $F_i$ by a bounded morphism. Conversely, with every finite rooted frame $F$ one associates a *canonical formula*. See Zakharyaschev [459] and Chagrov-Zakharyaschev [86, Chapter 9].
